# Contrast, not hue, drives food-app safety

For a UK grocery and meal-planning app whose core promise is per-person dietary safety, the evidence supports a **light, near-white page tinted toward the brand hue at imperceptible chroma (OKLCH L 0.985, C 0.004), white cards, near-black ink, one saturated brand colour reserved for actions, and three semantic states separated by lightness and shape rather than by hue alone** — and it does not support a warm cream canvas. The well-replicated findings are narrow: dark text on a light background reads faster than the reverse, especially for small text ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)); button findability depends on figure–ground contrast, not on which hue the button is ([NN/g eye-tracking, n=71](https://nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty)); traffic-light coding raises comprehension of food information in randomised tests ([Borgmeier & Westenhoefer 2009, n=420](https://doaj.org/article/c161dfccd96f462cbc2e0046c3fece1c)); and red warnings raise compliance ([Braun & Silver 1995](https://documentacion.fundacionmapfre.org/documentacion/publico/en/bib/49923.do)). Almost everything else in popular colour advice — "blue suppresses appetite", "red converts 21% better", "blue means trust", "cream is easier on the eyes" — is unreplicated, confounded or simply untested, and a 2026 KDD replication programme found published UI-pattern uplifts to be "highly exaggerated" ([Vermeer, Kohavi et al.](https://www.lukasvermeer.nl/publications/paper/2026/09/02/trustworthy-ab-patterns-and-the-winners-curse.html)). The binding constraints are therefore not psychological but regulatory and perceptual: WCAG 2.2 AA (4.5:1 text, 3:1 components), colour never as the sole carrier of meaning, UK allergen law that mandates *typographic* emphasis of the 14 allergens, and the fact that roughly 8% of men cannot separate red from green or amber ([NHS patient leaflet](https://www.uhsussex.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/2214-Colour-vision-deficiency.pdf)). The report closes with a full token specification — a deep plum brand (#752B69, 9.11:1 with white text), a solid dark-red block (#A70511, 7.86:1), a dark-text amber caution (#FDBE45, 10.21:1) and a pale-container safe state (#DCF8EA with #005F3E text, 6.89:1) — with every contrast figure computed and a colour-vision-deficiency simulation showing why "safe" must be light and "block" must be dark.

## Hue folklore collapses under replication; contrast, congruence and saturation survive

The single most important thing the evidence says about colour and conversion is that the famous hue results do not hold up. HubSpot's "red beats green by 21%" was one test on one page where red was the only red element on a green-dominated layout, so it measured isolation, not hue, and no confidence interval or stopping rule was published ([Poper summary](https://www.poper.ai/blog/cta-button-color-conversion/)). Google's "41 shades of blue" was a link-colour test at billions of impressions whose "$200M" figure came from an executive's conference anecdote rather than a methodology ([Manchester Digital](https://www.manchesterdigital.com/post/manchester-digital/why-google-has-200m-reasons-to-put-engineers-over-designers)). VWO's own red-button case admits the sales lift was not significant ([VWO](https://vwo.com/blog/the-attack-of-red-buttons-how-gsm-nl-reduced-bounce-rate-by-doing-a-simple-change/)). The decisive evidence is the KDD 2026 programme by Vermeer, Kohavi and GoodUI's own founder Linowski: eight pre-registered A/B tests at a **median 2.4 million users each** found pattern-library effects "substantially smaller than previously claimed", with only two of eight significant in the expected direction and one reversed ([Vermeer et al.](https://www.lukasvermeer.nl/publications/paper/2026/09/02/trustworthy-ab-patterns-and-the-winners-curse.html); [arXiv 2512.24521](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.24521v2)). What does replicate is mechanical: in NN/g's eye-tracking of 71 users, pages with weak clickability signifiers (low contrast, no containment) cost **22% more time and 25% more fixations** ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty)), and a separate lab study found users fixate a button faster when it contrasts with its surroundings ([Bartoš & Habarta](https://dspace5old.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/36165/1/2_Bartos_Habarta.pdf)). For a basket-approval step this means the approve action needs to be the single highest-chroma, highest-contrast element on the screen; which hue it is matters far less than whether anything else competes with it.

The appetite literature is in similar shape. Charles Spence's 2021 review concludes the blue-steak aversion anecdote "may not even have been scientifically validated" and notes blue has become a *successful* colour in drinks and confectionery ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.638703/full)). Genschow's red-plate-reduces-eating result failed replication twice ([Appalachian State 2013](https://honors.appstate.edu/node/353); [Doležalová 2021, n=148](https://muni.cz/en/research/publications/1786597)), and the FORRT atlas records one failed replication ([FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1016/j.appet.2011.12.023/)). The wider "red priming" literature from Elliot and Maier has no evidential value after publication-bias correction in a 67-effect meta-analysis ([Gnambs 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01772-1)), and the Mehta & Zhu red/blue cognition claim failed a replication with triple the sample ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/video/failure-to-replicate-the-mehta-and-zhu-2009-color-effect.html)). The most directly relevant screen study is Schlintl & Schienle (2020): across 448 women, recolouring food images in *any* non-natural colour (including red) lowered propensity to eat, while **coloured backgrounds had no effect on wanting** (Experiment 2, n=231) ([PMC7658407](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7658407/)). The one screen-based lever that does work is photographic saturation: higher saturation of a food photo on a simulated ordering platform made it look fresher and tastier and raised willingness to order (n=267), more so for distant shots ([Liu et al. 2022](https://www.technologynetworks.com/applied-sciences/news/image-color-saturation-can-have-a-big-impact-on-foods-appeal-364270)). The practical ordering is therefore: never recolour or tint the food itself, keep imagery saturated and on a neutral stage, and stop worrying about whether the chrome is "appetising".

Semantic associations, as distinct from appetite, are real but learned. Red maps to "tasty" and green to "healthy" in matched UK and Chinese samples (n=200) ([Luo et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/144640/)) and in Germany (n=222), where **light blue specifically lowered perceived healthiness via an association with artificial additives** ([HAW Hamburg](https://reposit.haw-hamburg.de/handle/20.500.12738/17128)); a Dutch tasting study independently found light blue a liability for food packaging ([WUR](https://www.resource-online.nl/index.php/2017/04/06/red-packaging-better-for-light-products/?lang=en)). Pale "healthy" colours trigger a taste-inference backfire when tasting is impossible ([Mai et al. 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jouret/v92y2016i4p426-444.html)), and "au naturel" beige raised willingness to pay for healthy foods across seven studies but not for unhealthy ones ([Marozzo et al. 2020](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/23205/1/Effect%20of%20Au%20Naturel%20Packaging%20Color%20on%20WTP%20for%20Healthy%20Food_Forthcoming%20Psychology%20%26%20Marketing_2020.pdf)). Green also carries a health halo that survives scepticism: in a 700-participant German experiment consumers were "greenwashed by just a bio-typical product colour and high price" ([Journal of Consumer Policy](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10603-023-09537-8)). For a safety app the implication is ethical as much as aesthetic: green should mean "verified safe for this person" and nothing softer, so it cannot be read as a vague health endorsement.

Trust is where the gap between folklore and evidence is widest. Blue does rate as more trustworthy than black, red or green on first impression (>200 participants, finance/legal/medical sites), but the authors call the effect "statistically significant but limited" ([Alberts & van der Geest 2011](https://makinggood.ac.nz/a/alberts,-w-a,-van-der-geest,-t-m-(2011,-may)/)), and a Colour Culture and Science paper calls the blue-trust postulate methodologically unreliable ([CCSJ](https://jcolore.gruppodelcolore.it/ojs/index.php/CCSJ/article/view/290)). The only food-specific digital trust experiment found (frozen-food packs shown online, n=220) ranked **green and yellow, not blue, highest for trust**, with yellow best for freshness ([University of Bari](https://ricerca.uniba.it/handle/11586/545621)). Fogg's credibility study of 2,684 consumers found "design look" the largest single credibility cue, but colour was never coded separately ([ClickZ](https://clickz.com/study-for-site-cred-looks-matter/56564/)). Trust in a food app is plausibly a freshness-risk and competence problem, not a hue problem; it is earned by specific, sourced allergen statements and a clean, familiar layout, which is consistent with Tuch et al.'s finding that prototypicality and low visual complexity dominate appeal within 17 ms ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/)).

Readability is the best-evidenced area and the one most often misquoted. Positive polarity (dark on light) beats negative polarity for proofreading and acuity across Buchner & Baumgartner, Piepenbrock and Dobres, and the advantage grows as text shrinks and at night ([NN/g review](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/); [Mannheim repository](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/26118/)). Users do not *perceive* the difference, which is why stated dark-mode preference of 81.9% in a self-selected poll ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/dark-mode-poll-results-1090716)) coexists with measured light-mode superiority. Crucially for the cream question, the one direct tablet test of white versus buff versus sepia-on-buff (100 participants, UK and Thailand) found **no performance difference on any measure** ([Chatrangsan & Petrie 2019](https://www.scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2019/77378)), and the dyslexia study usually cited for warm backgrounds (Rello & Bigham, n=341) did not include pure white, so it shows warm beats *cool*, not warm beats white ([ASSETS 2017](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)). ## Top-rated apps run one saturated hue on a white stage, and the UK hue map is nearly full

The audit of what shipping products actually do is thinner than one would like — most app backgrounds are undocumented — but the documented cases point one way. Sainsbury's is the only UK grocer with a public design system: its Luna foundations specify brand orange **#F06C00**, and all its digital products use "components and tiles in white on pale grey" ([Luna](https://luna.sainsburys.co.uk/foundations/colour/sainsburys-colours/); [Creativepool](https://creativepool.com/sainsburys/projects/sainsburys-digital-experience-for-sainsburys)). Deliveroo's product UI is built on teal **#00CCBC** and described by a token scrape as "light, dense, cool, bright" ([Fonts In Use](https://fontsinuse.com/uses/14757/deliveroo); [webdesignhot](https://www.webdesignhot.com/design.md/deliveroo/)). Uber Eats runs a near black-and-white palette with one green accent ([superdesign.dev](https://superdesign.dev/blog/uber-design-system)). MyFitnessPal's sampled tokens show a white canvas ([webdesignhot](https://www.webdesignhot.com/design.md/myfitnesspal/)). No food app in scope was found with a dark default. The cream exceptions are brand-led and photography-light: Instacart's sampled **bg-cream #F7F4EE** is a *tertiary* surface, not the page ([webdesignhot](https://www.webdesignhot.com/design.md/instacart/)); Mindful Chef's "Oat – a warm off-white that introduces calm" sits against aubergine in an editorial meal-kit identity ([LBB](https://lbbonline.com/news/Mindful-Chef-Mother-Design-Rebrand)); Headspace's "soft cream surfaces" belong to an illustration-led, imagery-free product ([webdesignhot Headspace](https://www.webdesignhot.com/design.md/headspace/)). Award recognition in the period went to minimal, type-led, interaction-led recipe apps — Crouton (ADA 2024 Interaction) and Mela (ADA 2025 finalist) — praised for "clean" and "simple-but-stunning" interfaces rather than for colour ([TapSmart](https://www.tapsmart.com/news/design-awards-2024/); [Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/06/apple-unveils-winners-and-finalists-of-the-2025-apple-design-awards/)); MacroFactor took Google Play's 2024 Best Everyday Essential in the UK ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20241118746780/en/MacroFactor-Wins-Google-Play-Best-of-2024-Award)).

The allergy and nutrition scanners that most resemble the proposed product converge on the same grammar: Fig returns "a green check for safe, a yellow circle for caution, or a red X for avoid" and routes a "does not fit" result to alternatives rather than a dead end ([screensdesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/fig-food-scanner-discovery)); Spoonful gives "a clear green, yellow, or red result" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1481914232)); Yuka bands scores dark-green/light-green/orange/red ([WCRF](https://www.wcrf.org/about-us/news-and-blogs/healthy-new-you-the-yuka-app-review/)). This means UK users will arrive expecting green/amber/red to mean safe/caution/avoid, which in turn makes **red, amber and green unavailable as a brand colour** for any app that surfaces per-person verdicts. Cronometer's rebrand is the one documented case of a tracker adding colours "to make the app more accessibility friendly" ([Cronometer](https://cronometer.com/blog/our-new-look/)).

The UK supermarket hue map leaves little room and explains the differentiation moves of recent entrants. Blue belongs to Tesco (aggregators disagree between #00539F and #005DAA), Lidl (#0050AA) and Aldi (#001E5E); red to Tesco, Lidl, Aldi, Picnic and Gousto; orange to Sainsbury's, Just Eat and Instacart; yellow to Morrisons, Lidl and Aldi; green to Asda (#78BE21), Morrisons, Waitrose, Uber Eats, Too Good To Go and HelloFresh ([brandcolorcode Tesco](https://www.brandcolorcode.com/tesco-mobile); [Lidl](https://www.brandcolorcode.com/lidl); [Aldi](https://www.brandcolorcode.com/aldi); [Asda](https://brandpalettes.com/asda-logo-colors/)). Ocado's 2021 switch from green to purple "Grape" was explicitly to escape "a market crowded with greens" ([Creative Boom](https://www.creativeboom.com/news/jkr-redesigns-online-grocery-behemoth-ocado/)); Olio left green because it is "more than just a sustainability app" ([Olio](https://olioapp.com/en/olio-updates/meet-the-new-look-olio/)); Getir took purple and yellow as "basically unclaimed" ([ben.page](https://ben.page/getir-colors)). Monzo's hot coral and Airbnb's Rausch were justified entirely on positioning and warmth, not testing; the only testing language any of these brands publish is Monzo's note that secondary colours were "carefully selected to maximize accessibility" ([Monzo community](https://community.monzo.com/t/we-ve-given-our-visual-brand-a-refresh-q-a-w-jesse-senior-brand-designer/139681)). On this evidence the least-occupied, non-semantic hue families in UK food are deep aubergine/plum (Mindful Chef only) and pink/magenta, which is why the specification below uses a deep plum.

Across the leading design systems the construction method is consistent and worth copying rather than the colours themselves. Material 3 builds every scheme from one seed hue with fixed chroma per role — primary 36, neutral **6**, neutral-variant 8 on the HCT scale — and sets surface at tone 98, primary at tone 40 and text at tone 10 ([scheme_tonal_spot.dart](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/main/dart/lib/scheme/scheme_tonal_spot.dart); [color_spec_2021.ts](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/main/typescript/dynamiccolor/color_spec_2021.ts)). Radix fixes step semantics (1–2 page, 3–5 component states, 6–8 borders, 9 the one high-chroma solid, 11–12 text guaranteed to APCA Lc 60 and 90 on step 2) ([Radix](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/radix-ui/website/main/data/colors/docs/palette-composition/understanding-the-scale.mdx)); Tailwind v4 ships its whole palette in OKLCH ([Tailwind](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tailwindlabs/tailwindcss.com/main/src/docs/colors.mdx)); Carbon and GOV.UK stay near-achromatic on #ffffff or #f4f4f4 ([Carbon](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/main/src/pages/elements/color/overview.mdx)). None of the major systems defaults to a visibly cream page; where warmth exists it is a tint of a few chroma units in the brand hue. Practitioner consensus is the same — "lean your greys warm or cool, but only slightly", two to three percent of brand hue in every neutral ([Refactoring UI summary](https://playbooks.com/skills/wondelai/skills/refactoring-ui); [UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/the-one-color-decision-that-makes-a-ui-look-expensive-d6890efe11ba)) — but it is craft consensus, not measured preference. The cautionary trend data is that both the indigo-gradient "AI slop" look and the reflexive cream-plus-serif "paper" look are now recognised templates; Tailwind's founder publicly apologised for the former ([prg.sh](https://prg.sh/ramblings/Why-Your-AI-Keeps-Building-the-Same-Purple-Gradient-Website)), and 2026 commentary describes cream as "the default tasteful AI surface, reached for by reflex" ([noqta.tn](https://noqta.tn/en/blog/ai-design-slop-overused-ui-patterns-fix-2026)).

## WCAG 2.2 AA is the floor, UK law mandates bold not colour, and 1 in 12 men cannot see your traffic light

The enforceable bar for a UK consumer app is WCAG 2.2 AA: **4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text (24px, or 18.66px bold) and 3:1 for component boundaries, states and meaningful graphics**, computed from sRGB relative luminance, with no rounding — "4.499:1 would not meet the 4.5:1 threshold" ([W3C Understanding 1.4.3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/w3c/wcag/main/understanding/20/contrast-minimum.html); [1.4.11](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/w3c/wcag/main/understanding/21/non-text-contrast.html)). AAA lifts text to 7:1, chosen to compensate for roughly 20/80 vision ([1.4.6](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/w3c/wcag/main/understanding/20/contrast-enhanced.html)); the NHS says it aims for AAA "as far as possible" ([NHS service manual](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nhsuk/nhsuk-service-manual/main/app/views/design-system/styles/colour/index.njk)). Success criterion 1.4.1 forbids colour as the only distinguishing cue and names "errors shown only in red" as a failure; where users must perceive a *particular* colour, an additional indicator is required regardless of contrast ([1.4.1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/w3c/wcag/main/understanding/20/use-of-color.html)). Two subtleties matter for a basket-approval flow. First, 1.4.11 exempts *inactive* components, so an allergen block must not be implemented as a merely disabled button; it needs to be an active state with its own ≥3:1 indicator and text. Second, APCA — the WCAG 3 candidate, still non-normative — disagrees with WCAG 2 precisely on light text over orange: white on GOV.UK orange #f47738 scores 2.78:1 (fails AA even for large text) but APCA Lc −58 ([apca-w3](https://github.com/Myndex/apca-w3); [APCA in a Nutshell](https://github.com/Myndex/SAPC-APCA/blob/master/documentation/APCA_in_a_Nutshell.md)). The safe policy is to pass both: use dark text on amber, and treat APCA as a tie-breaker among WCAG-passing candidates.

Colour vision deficiency sets the second hard constraint. Congenital red–green CVD affects about 8% of men and 0.4–1% of women of European descent, with deuteranomaly (≈5% of men) the largest group ([NHS leaflet](https://www.uhsussex.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/2214-Colour-vision-deficiency.pdf); [PMC12385717](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12385717/)). These observers struggle with red vs green, orange vs red and green vs brown, and see chromatic colour as roughly two hues, yellow and blue ([NIST](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/041/4/V41.N04.A01.pdf)). A naïve safe/caution/block system of mid-green, amber and red therefore sits entirely on the confusion axis. The accepted mitigations are to let lightness do the work, to prefer blue-versus-orange oppositions, and to attach a shape and a word to every state ([AudioEye](https://www.audioeye.com/post/colorblind-friendly-palettes/)). The NHS adds a finding that refines the usual "add an icon" advice: in its research, clearly worded headings "are better than icons, like exclamation marks, which may not be accessible and can be interpreted in different ways", and "people with visual disabilities may not recognise care cards by their colour" ([NHS warning callout](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nhsuk/nhsuk-service-manual/main/app/views/design-system/components/warning-callout/index.njk); [NHS care cards](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nhsuk/nhsuk-service-manual/main/app/views/design-system/patterns/help-users-decide-when-and-where-to-get-care/index.njk)). The robust redundant channel is a specific text label; colour and icon are reinforcement.

The safety-colour grammar is fixed across standards and should be adopted, not reinvented. ISO 3864 and ISO 7010 reserve red for prohibition and danger, yellow for warning, blue for mandatory action and green for safe condition, each bound to a shape (red circle-and-slash, yellow triangle, blue circle, green square) so the message survives loss of colour ([ISO 3864](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_3864); [ISO 7010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_7010)); ISO 3864-2 grades severity yellow/orange/red for low/medium/high risk. ANSI Z535 pairs DANGER with white-on-red and CAUTION with black-on-yellow ([In Compliance](https://incompliancemag.com/ansi-z535-1-safety-colors-in-focus/)). The US FDA standardises **red as the "Allergy" wristband colour** in hospitals ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/industry-medical-devices/use-purple-bracelets-or-wristbands-only-do-not-resuscitate-status-letter-industry)). UK consumers already read red/amber/green on front-of-pack nutrition labels, which the government guidance stresses "do not represent claims" ([FoP guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80cd03ed915d74e33fc7c5/FoP_Nutrition_labelling_UK_guidance.pdf)). GOV.UK Frontend fixes error red #d4351c, success green #00703c, focus yellow #ffdd00 with black, and text #0b0c0c on white ([govuk-frontend](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/alphagov/govuk-frontend/v5.9.0/packages/govuk-frontend/src/govuk/settings/_colours-palette.scss)); the NHS uses red #d5281b, green #007f3b, yellow #ffeb3b and text #212b32 on a grey-5 page #f0f4f5 chosen "because it reduces overall page glare" and because the British Dyslexia Association recommends "dark text on a light, but not white, background" ([NHS colour](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nhsuk/nhsuk-service-manual/main/app/views/design-system/styles/colour/index.njk)). Neither UK system uses orange semantically, and both bind every colour to a visible label and a hidden screen-reader prefix. Apple's guidance states the over-use risk directly: "a red triangle that warns people of a critical problem becomes less effective when red is used elsewhere in an app for noncritical reasons" ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color)).

UK allergen law fixes content and timing but deliberately not colour. The 14 regulated allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list "in bold, CAPITAL LETTERS, contrasting colours or underlined", every time they appear, and since 1 October 2021 (Natasha's Law) on all pre-packed-for-direct-sale food ([RBWM Trading Standards](https://rbwm.gov.uk/business-and-economy/trading-standards/natashas-law); [FSA](https://www.food.gov.uk/business-industry/guidancenotes/food-sold-online)). Food sold through an app must present allergen information **before purchase and again at delivery** ([FSA technical guidance](https://www.food.gov.uk/business-guidance/food-allergen-labelling-and-information-requirements-technical-guidance-part-3-guidance-for-businesses-providing-food-prepacked)). The FSA's 2023 evaluation of 1,809 consumers found people attribute better decisions to bold emphasis — "if there's nothing in bold, I know it's got nothing in that's wheat" ([FSA PPDS evaluation](https://www.food.gov.uk/research/food-hypersensitivity/evaluation-of-the-implementation-of-prepacked-for-direct-sale-ppds-allergen-labelling-requirements)) — which means any ingredient list rendered without bold allergens is a safety defect, not a styling choice. Precautionary "may contain" wording, by contrast, is distrusted: the FSA's 2022 consultation (>2,500 responses) found PAL "confusing", "applied inconsistently" and "too vague to support informed decision making" ([FSA PAL report](https://www.food.gov.uk/sites/default/files/media/document/PAL_Consultation%20report_Final_May%202022%201.pdf)). The design consequence is two visually distinct registers — a definite "contains X" block and a precautionary "may contain X" caution that names the allergen and the risk — rather than one undifferentiated warning. "Gluten free" may be claimed only at ≤20 ppm, and the Crossed Grain symbol is a licensed trademark that must not be imitated ([Coeliac UK](https://coeliac.org.uk/food-industry-professionals/the-crossed-grain-symbol)), so a coeliac-safe state should use the app's own safe treatment plus the words, not a lookalike mark.

Finally, the habituation literature governs how the block must be deployed. fMRI shows "a dramatic drop in the visual processing centers of the brain after only the second exposure to a warning" ([BYU](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/9306)), and in a three-week field experiment adherence to mobile permission warnings "substantially decreased", while polymorphic warnings kept adherence about 20 percentage points higher ([MIS Quarterly 2018](https://misq.org/tuning-out-security-warnings-a-longitudinal-examination-of-habituation-through-fmri-eye-tracking-and-field-experiments.html)). GOV.UK's rule that red error messages must not be used for problems the user cannot fix, and that such cases should instead "explain the problem … and what to do next" ([GOV.UK error message](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/main/src/components/error-message/index.md)), together with the NHS limit of two callouts per page "to reassure people, not to scare them", point to a block that is rare, reserved in colour and shape, specific ("Contains peanuts — Maya's allergy"), sourced, and paired with a next action. Red used anywhere else in the app — sale badges, validation errors, brand accents — spends the signal the block depends on.

## Verdict on cream: near-white tinted, never a yellow-shifted canvas

The question of warm cream versus near-white can now be answered with numbers rather than taste. The evidence for cream's benefits is absent or confounded: no study compares #FFFFFF with an off-white on an emissive phone screen; the direct buff-versus-white tablet test found no difference ([Chatrangsan & Petrie](https://www.scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2019/77378)); the warm-background advantage in Rello & Bigham is relative to cool colours, with lightness confounded ([ASSETS 2017](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)); and "never use pure white" posts cite no controlled comparison ([UX Movement](https://uxmovement.com/content/why-you-should-never-use-pure-black-for-text-or-backgrounds/)). The NHS's choice of a *grey* off-white (#f0f4f5, cool-tinted, OKLCH L 0.964) for glare and dyslexia reasons is the strongest institutional precedent for "not pure white", and it is not cream. Against that, cream carries three measurable costs for this product. It reduces contrast for every mid-tone token: the AA-minimum grey #767676 falls from 4.54:1 on white to **4.22:1 on #FAF6EF and fails** ([computed in the standards notes from the W3C formula](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/w3c/wcag/main/guidelines/terms/20/relative-luminance.html)). It flattens the card-on-page hierarchy that Sainsbury's and Material rely on, because white tiles on cream read as a colour difference rather than a lift. And it skews the hue relationships of the red/amber/green status set on a yellow ground, while fighting the neutral stage that saturated food photography needs. The reasoning is not that warmth is wrong — M3's neutral at chroma 6, Airbnb's warm #222222 ink and the practitioner "two to three percent of brand hue" rule all endorse a whisper of warmth — but that a *visible* cream such as #FFFDD0 (OKLCH chroma 0.058) is a named colour, now a recognised template, and an engineering liability.

The computed comparison below uses the report's own secondary-text and status tokens so the trade-off is concrete. Moving from pure white to the recommended near-white costs almost nothing (secondary text 6.89:1 → 6.59:1); moving to Instacart's cream costs a further 0.3 ratio points and 3 Lc on every pair, and the pale-card lift drops to 1.10:1. The recommended page is therefore **#FCF9FB — OKLCH(0.985 0.004 337)** — tinted toward the plum brand hue at a chroma below the threshold of being named a colour, with pure white reserved for cards so hierarchy comes from tone, not tint.

Warm off-whites in the #F7F4EE–#FAF6EF range are darker *and* tinted, so they lose on both counts.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the brief. A team that set out to find the "right" colours for trust and appetite will find that the literature cannot supply them, because hue effects on behaviour are small, learned, culturally variable and mostly unreplicated; what the literature can supply is a short list of constraints that are strong, measurable and in several cases legally or physiologically fixed. Treat colour as an engineering material governed by lightness, contrast, reserved semantics and redundancy, put the brand's personality into a single accent, typography and photography, and the app will look like the best-rated products in its category — which are, on inspection, exactly that: one saturated hue on a white stage.

The sharpest novel finding is the one the simulation produced. A conventional solid-green "safe" badge and a solid-red "block" badge, each of which passes WCAG with white text, are **indistinguishable in lightness (1.01:1) to a deuteranope** — roughly one male user in twenty — even though they look nothing alike to everyone else. Rendering "safe" as a pale container and "block" as the only dark solid in the system restores a 5.3:1 lightness gap without changing hue, and that structural decision, more than any particular hex value, is what makes a per-person safety promise honest. The specification that follows is built around it.

## Palette specification and the reasoning behind each value

All tokens were derived in OKLCH, converted to sRGB, and checked with the W3C relative-luminance ratio ([W3C glossary](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/w3c/wcag/main/guidelines/terms/20/relative-luminance.html)) and the apca-w3 0.1.9 constants ([Myndex](https://github.com/Myndex/apca-w3)); the calculator reproduced the standards notes' worked examples (#767676 on #FAF6EF = 4.22:1; white on #f47738 = 2.78:1, Lc −58.3) to within rounding. Colour-vision simulations use the Viénot–Brettel–Mollon (1999) protanope and deuteranope matrices on linear sRGB; treat all figures as ±0.05 ratio points / ±1 Lc and re-verify in the production pipeline. Hex values are what shipped code should use; the OKLCH form is the design intent and should be the source of truth if the ramp is regenerated.

**Neutral family (plum-tinted, chroma 0.004–0.020).** The neutrals follow Material 3's method of tinting toward the brand hue at a chroma too low to name ([scheme_tonal_spot.dart](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/main/dart/lib/scheme/scheme_tonal_spot.dart)), which gives the just-perceptible warmth practitioners recommend without a cream cast. The page sits at L 0.985 (Material's surface tone 98 equivalent); cards are pure white so they lift off the page the way Sainsbury's white tiles do; containers step down in tone for hierarchy instead of using a second hue.

**Brand (deep plum).** Plum is chosen by elimination: red, amber and green are spent on semantics and are owned by Tesco, Lidl, Aldi, Sainsbury's, Just Eat, Asda, Morrisons and Waitrose; blue is Tesco/Lidl/Aldi territory and light blue signals "artificial" in food; orange and teal belong to Sainsbury's and Deliveroo; purple is Ocado's but at a brighter "Grape" register. Deep plum at L 0.42 is the only hue family with a single occupant (Mindful Chef's aubergine), and it carries a second structural advantage: it lies on the *blue* side of the red–green confusion lines, so protan and deutan observers see it as blue-grey (#3F3F69 / #4D4D67 in simulation) while the block red collapses to olive (#4F4F16 / #6A6A00) — the two hues such observers can tell apart. It is dark enough for white text at AAA, which keeps the primary CTA readable under both WCAG 2 and APCA without the orange-button problem.

**Usage rules that the evidence makes non-negotiable.** Red appears nowhere except the block tokens, so the signal does not habituate; the block always states the specific allergen, the person, the data source and a next action, and is never a disabled control. Allergens in any reproduced ingredient list are rendered in bold text regardless of colour, because UK law and FSA research make bold the channel consumers rely on. Food photography is never tinted, overlaid or desaturated, and sits on #FFFFFF or #FCF9FB only. Brand plum is the only colour for primary actions, so the basket "Approve" button is always the highest-contrast element on the screen. Every semantic state ships with a border in its text colour, an ISO-derived icon shape and a word, and the design is tested with a CVD simulator and with users before release, as the NHS manual requires. Nothing in this specification should be A/B tested for hue at low power; if the team wants to test colour at all, the Vermeer–Kohavi standard of pre-registered minimum detectable effects and ≥80% power is the bar, and the expected effect of any hue change is close to zero.
